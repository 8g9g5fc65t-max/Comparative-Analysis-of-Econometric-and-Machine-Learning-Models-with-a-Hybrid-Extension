\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================
\subsection{Motivation}
% ============================================================
Financial market volatility is an important component of quantitative finance and risk management.
Accurate volatility forecasts are relevant for the measurement of market risk, portfolio management
and financial decision-making. Traditional econometric models, particularly models from the ARCH
and GARCH families, have been widely used to model the time-varying nature of financial volatility.

In recent years, machine learning methods have become increasingly relevant in financial forecasting.
Their ability to model nonlinear relationships provides a potential alternative to traditional
econometric approaches. However, greater predictive flexibility does not necessarily imply better
out-of-sample forecasts or more accurate measures of financial risk.

This work therefore compares traditional econometric volatility models with machine learning models
using a common out-of-sample forecasting framework. The analysis is subsequently extended to a
hybrid model combining econometric and machine learning approaches. The final objective is to
determine whether improvements in volatility forecasting translate into improvements in market-risk
measurement.
% ============================================================
\subsection{Research Question}
% ============================================================
This project will try to answer the two following questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Can machine-learning methods improve the out-of-sample forecasting of financial market volatility compared with traditional econometric models, and does any improvement translate into more accurate market-risk measures?
    \item Does combining GARCH and machine learning provide additional forecasting value relative to the individual models?
\end{enumerate}
% ============================================================
\subsection{Objectives}
% ============================================================
The main objective of this work is to evaluate whether machine-learning models provide improvements
in the out-of-sample forecasting of financial market volatility relative to traditional econometric
models, and whether these differences translate into more accurate market-risk measures.

A secondary objective is to develop a hybrid GARCH--machine learning model and determine whether
combining the two approaches provides additional forecasting value compared with the corresponding
individual models.

The objective is not to assume that machine learning models are superior, but to evaluate their
performance under a common and strictly out-of-sample framework.
% ============================================================
\subsection{Contributions}
% ============================================================
The main contribution of this work is an empirical comparison between traditional econometric
volatility models and machine learning models using the same financial data, forecasting horizon
and out-of-sample evaluation criteria.

The analysis is complemented by a market-risk application through the estimation and backtesting
of Value at Risk (VaR) and Expected Shortfall (ES). Finally, a hybrid GARCH--machine learning model
is developed to evaluate whether the combination of econometric and machine learning information
provides an additional improvement in forecasting performance.

\section{Literature Review}
% ============================================================
The literature relevant to this thesis can be organised into four themes: the
econometric foundations of volatility modelling, the measurement of market risk,
the evaluation of volatility forecasts, and the more recent application of machine
learning and hybrid approaches to the same problem.

The econometric treatment of time-varying volatility originates with
Engle (1982), who introduced the ARCH model to capture the clustering of
volatility observed in macroeconomic and financial time series. Bollerslev (1986)
generalised this framework with the GARCH model, allowing conditional variance to
depend on its own lagged values in addition to lagged squared shocks. Together
these two papers established conditional heteroskedasticity models as the standard
econometric benchmark for volatility forecasting, and GARCH(1,1) in particular
remains difficult to outperform consistently in out-of-sample comparisons. This
thesis extends the baseline GARCH specification with an asymmetric GJR-GARCH
variant, motivated by the well-documented tendency of volatility to react more
strongly to negative than to positive returns.

Once volatility is forecast, it must be translated into a usable measure of
market risk. Jorion (1997) formalised Value at Risk as an industry and regulatory
benchmark, while Artzner et al. (1999) showed that VaR lacks subadditivity and is
therefore not a coherent risk measure, motivating the use of Expected Shortfall as
a complementary, coherent alternative. This thesis follows that recommendation by
reporting both VaR and Expected Shortfall rather than VaR alone.

A separate strand of the literature addresses how volatility forecasts themselves
should be evaluated. Poon and Granger (2003) review the difficulties inherent in
this exercise, in particular the sensitivity of forecast rankings to the choice of
realised-volatility proxy and evaluation loss function, and stress the importance
of genuinely out-of-sample testing. This motivates the use of multiple loss
functions (MAE, RMSE and QLIKE) in the present work rather than a single metric,
since no individual measure is uniformly preferred.

More recently, machine learning methods have been applied to volatility
forecasting as an alternative to purely econometric specifications. Gunnarsson et
al. (2024) survey this literature and highlight two recurring methodological
concerns: the risk of data leakage when constructing features from
information not genuinely available at the forecasting date, and the tendency of
flexible models to overfit in relatively short financial samples. Misra et al.
(2025) provide a direct comparison between GARCH, XGBoost and LSTM models for
S\&P 500 volatility forecasting, finding that the relative ranking of models is
sensitive to the forecast horizon and evaluation criterion used. Neither the
survey nor the comparative study establishes a uniform conclusion in favour of
machine learning over econometric models, which motivates the neutral,
criteria-driven comparison adopted in this thesis rather than an a priori
assumption of superiority.

A smaller body of work combines the two approaches directly. Kartsonakis
Mademlis and Dritsakis (2021) compare standalone GARCH and neural network models
against a hybrid specification for the Italian stock market and report gains from
the hybrid approach, though without a fully out-of-sample evaluation matched
across models. This result motivates the hybrid extension developed later in this
thesis, in which a machine learning model is trained specifically on the residual
information left unexplained by GARCH, rather than combined with it in an
unstructured way.

Taken together, this literature establishes GARCH-family models as the relevant
econometric benchmark, VaR and Expected Shortfall as complementary risk measures,
and highlights both the promise and the methodological pitfalls of applying
machine learning to volatility forecasting. What remains comparatively unexplored
is a single, strictly out-of-sample comparison that carries econometric,
machine-learning and hybrid volatility forecasts through to their implications
for backtested market-risk measures — which is the gap this thesis addresses.

\section{Data}
% ============================================================
\subsection{Dataset}
% ============================================================
The analysis uses approximately fifteen years of daily S\&P 500 data, covering the period from
January 2011 to 7 July 2026. Data is extracted using the \texttt{yfinance} package (ticker
\texttt{\^{}GSPC}) and the end date is fixed at 7 July 2026 so that the dataset (and therefore all
downstream results) remains identical across every re-run of the pipeline.

The dataset contains daily Open, High, Low, Close and Volume observations. Adjusted closing prices
are used for the calculation of daily returns; since the S\&P 500 is a price index rather than a
total-return index, adjusted and unadjusted closing prices coincide, and no dividend adjustment is
required at the index level.

The raw data is cleaned by removing duplicate entries and any weekend dates accidentally included
by the data source. Rather than deleting anomalous records -- such as days with missing information,
zero trading volume, or extreme market swings -- the cleaning script preserves these rows and flags
them for review, so that no information is silently discarded. Daily logarithmic returns are then
calculated to prepare the data for modelling.

Two flagged events were identified in the sample:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{16 March 2020} (log return $-12.8\%$): corresponds to the COVID-19 market crash
    and reflects a genuine market event rather than a data error.
    \item \textbf{24 May 2023} (zero trading volume): likely a data-provider reporting quirk; the
    price data for this day appears otherwise consistent.
\end{itemize}

After cleaning, the dataset comprises 3,899 daily observations.
% ============================================================
\subsection{Returns}
% ============================================================
Daily logarithmic returns are calculated as

\begin{equation}
r_t = \ln\left(\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}}\right),
\end{equation}
where $P_t$ represents the adjusted closing price of the S\&P 500 on day $t$.
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{results/figures/returns_timeseries.pdf}
\caption{Daily logarithmic returns of the S\&P 500, 2011--2026. The 16 March 2020 COVID-19 crash is
annotated; periods of high and low volatility cluster together rather than occurring randomly,
motivating the use of conditional heteroskedasticity models.}
\label{fig:returns_timeseries}
\end{figure}

% ============================================================
\subsection{Volatility Target}\label{sec:target}
% ============================================================
The dependent variable for the forecasting exercise is a five-day close-to-close realised
volatility measure.

At each date $t$, the realised variance over the following five trading days is calculated as

\begin{equation}
RV_{t+1:t+5} =
\sum_{i=1}^{5} r_{t+i}^{2}.
\end{equation}

The corresponding realised volatility is defined as

\begin{equation}
\sqrt{\frac{252}{5}RV_{t+1:t+5}},
\end{equation}

where the factor $252$ annualises the volatility.\\
This measure provides a less noisy forecasting target than the squared return of a single trading
day while allowing the analysis to be performed entirely using daily data.

\section{Methodology}
% ============================================================
The methodology is designed to provide a common and strictly out-of-sample framework for comparing
econometric and machine learning approaches.

All models forecast the same five-day volatility target using only information available at
the forecasting date.

\subsection{Out-of-Sample Evaluation}\label{sec:oos}

The dataset is divided chronologically into a training period and an out-of-sample testing period:
observations from 3 January 2011 to 30 December 2022 (3,015 observations) are used for model
development, and observations from 3 January 2023 to 7 July 2026 (879 observations) are reserved
for the final out-of-sample evaluation.

A walk-forward expanding-window procedure is used rather than a random train-test split. At
each forecasting date, the model is estimated using information available up to that date and
subsequently produces a forecast for the following five trading days.

This procedure avoids look-ahead bias and provides a realistic assessment of the models' forecasting
performance.

\subsection{Econometric Models}

Three econometric approaches are considered.

\subsubsection{EWMA}

Exponentially Weighted Moving Average volatility is used as a simple benchmark. EWMA gives
greater weight to recent observations and provides a straightforward representation of volatility
clustering.

\subsubsection{GARCH(1,1)}

The standard GARCH(1,1) model is used as the main symmetric econometric benchmark. Its
conditional variance is specified as

\begin{equation}
\sigma_t^2 =
\omega +
\alpha \epsilon_{t-1}^2 +
\beta \sigma_{t-1}^2,
\end{equation}

where $\omega$, $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are model parameters.

\subsubsection{GJR-GARCH}

A GJR-GARCH model is included to account for potential asymmetry in the response of volatility
to positive and negative returns. This allows the analysis to determine whether explicitly modelling
asymmetric volatility improves forecasting performance relative to the standard GARCH model.

For comparability, all three econometric models are converted from a one-day-ahead conditional
variance forecast into the same five-day annualised volatility scale as the forecasting target
(Section~\ref{sec:target}), using an identical annualisation procedure applied uniformly across
models. GARCH(1,1) and GJR-GARCH produce a full five-step-ahead conditional variance path, which is
allowed to mean-revert towards its long-run level over the forecast horizon. EWMA, which has no
mean-reversion mechanism, instead applies its single-step variance forecast uniformly across all
five days -- the standard treatment for EWMA over a multi-day horizon. The three models therefore
differ not only in their conditional-variance dynamics but also in how that dynamic is extended
across the forecast horizon, a distinction worth bearing in mind when interpreting differences in
forecasting accuracy.

\subsection{Machine Learning Models}

Two machine learning algorithms are considered: Random Forest and XGBoost.

These models are selected because they can capture nonlinear relationships while remaining
substantially simpler to implement and interpret than more complex deep-learning architectures.

To keep the comparison against the econometric models uncontaminated, the machine learning models
used to answer Research Question 1 are trained using a limited set of six features constructed
exclusively from returns, without reference to any econometric model's own output:

Table~\ref{tab:ml_hyperparams} reports the hyperparameters used for both models. Both are configured

Restricting tree depth and, for Random Forest, requiring a minimum number of observations per leaf

patterns. XGBoost builds trees sequentially, with each new tree correcting the errors of the
previous one; a low learning rate (0.05) limits how aggressively the model adapts to any single

Gunnarsson et al. (2024) for machine learning applied to relatively short financial samples. A fixed
random seed (42) ensures the reported results are exactly reproducible.

Including the GARCH or EWMA forecasts as features at this stage would make the comparison circular:
any advantage attributed to machine learning could simply reflect access to the econometric model's
own output rather than a genuinely different source of predictive information. GARCH- and
EWMA-informed features are therefore introduced later, and only within the hybrid model
(Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}), where the machine learning component is explicitly tasked with modelling
the residual information left unexplained by GARCH rather than competing against it on equal terms.

All features are constructed exclusively from information available at the forecasting date, and
particular attention is paid to preventing data leakage during feature construction and model
training. The same features and forecasting target are used for both Random Forest and XGBoost to
ensure a homogeneous comparison.

Consistent with the walk-forward procedure described in Section~\ref{sec:oos}, the econometric
models are re-estimated daily, while Random Forest and XGBoost are re-trained weekly. Daily
re-training is inexpensive for the econometric models but unnecessary for the machine learning
models, whose more expensive fitting procedure does not require daily updating to remain reflective
of current market conditions.

\subsection{Volatility Forecast Evaluation}
% ============================================================
The volatility forecasts produced by all models are evaluated against the same five-day realised
volatility target. \\
The main evaluation metrics include Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE)
and the QLIKE loss function. MAE and RMSE are computed directly on the volatility scale:\\
\begin{equation}
\text{MAE} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N} \left| \hat{\sigma}_t - \sigma_t \right|,
\qquad
\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N} \left( \hat{\sigma}_t - \sigma_t \right)^2},
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_t$ is the realised volatility target and $\hat{\sigma}_t$ the model forecast.
QLIKE, by contrast, is defined on the variance scale and penalises underprediction of risk more
heavily than overprediction of the same magnitude, which makes it particularly informative in a
risk-management context:
\begin{equation}
\text{QLIKE} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N} \left[ \frac{\sigma_t^2}{\hat{\sigma}_t^2} - \ln\left(\frac{\sigma_t^2}{\hat{\sigma}_t^2}\right) - 1 \right].
\end{equation}

These three measures are complementary rather than interchangeable, and each answers a slightly
different question about forecast quality. MAE expresses the average forecasting error directly in
percentage points of annualised volatility: a MAE of $0.049$, for example, means that a model's
forecasts deviate from realised volatility by approximately 4.9 percentage points on an average day.
RMSE is expressed on the same scale but squares errors before averaging, so it is more heavily
influenced by occasional large misses than by many small ones; a model whose RMSE is noticeably
higher than its MAE is one that is usually close to the target but occasionally misses it by a wide
margin, which is the typical pattern for volatility forecasts around sudden market moves. QLIKE
cannot be read in percentage-point terms, since it is a ratio-based loss defined on variance rather
than volatility, but its economic interpretation is more directly relevant to risk management: it
penalises a forecast that understates risk far more severely than one that overstates it by the same
amount. This asymmetry matters because, in a risk-management context, telling a decision-maker that
volatility will remain low shortly before a spike is a materially more costly error than being overly
cautious ahead of a calm period -- a distinction that MAE and RMSE, which treat over- and
under-prediction symmetrically, do not capture.

Reporting all three measures avoids relying on a single loss function, since no individual metric is
uniformly preferred for evaluating volatility forecasts (Poon and Granger, 2003). These measures
provide complementary information about the accuracy of the forecasts and allow the performance of
the econometric, machine learning and hybrid models to be compared under the same criteria.

\subsection{Hybrid Model}\label{sec:hybrid}

The hybrid model will be developed after evaluating the standalone econometric and machine learning
models.

The purpose of the hybrid approach is not simply to combine model predictions mechanically, but to
give each component a defined role. The GARCH model will provide the baseline econometric volatility
forecast, while the machine learning component will attempt to model the remaining information not
captured by the GARCH specification.

Conceptually, the hybrid forecast will take the form

\begin{equation}
\hat{\sigma}^{Hybrid}_{t+1:t+5}
=
\hat{\sigma}^{GARCH}_{t+1:t+5}
+
\hat{e}^{ML}_{t+1:t+5}.
\end{equation}

where the machine learning component estimates the forecasting error associated with the econometric model.

The hybrid model will then be evaluated using exactly the same out-of-sample procedure and loss functions as the individual models.

\subsection{Market Risk Measures}\label{sec:market_risk}

The volatility forecasts are subsequently used to estimate market-risk measures.

Value at Risk is calculated for the selected confidence levels using a common distributional
assumption (parametric, Gaussian) across all models. Expected Shortfall is also calculated to
measure the expected loss conditional on exceeding the VaR threshold.

Using the same assumptions for all models ensures that differences in the resulting risk measures
are primarily attributable to differences in the volatility forecasts rather than to differences in
the risk-measurement methodology.

\subsection{Backtesting}\label{sec:backtesting}

The VaR forecasts are evaluated using standard backtesting procedures.

The Kupiec test is used to evaluate unconditional coverage, assessing whether the observed
frequency of VaR violations is consistent with the nominal confidence level.

The Christoffersen test is used to evaluate the independence and conditional coverage of VaR
violations.

Both tests are formulated as hypothesis tests, and it is the failure to reject the null hypothesis
that is desirable for a well-specified model. The Kupiec test's null hypothesis is that the observed
proportion of VaR violations equals the nominal confidence level (e.g. exactly 5\% of test days for a
95\% VaR); a high likelihood-ratio (LR) statistic and correspondingly low $p$-value indicate that the
violation rate is significantly too high or too low, and thus that the model is miscalibrated. The
Christoffersen test's null hypothesis is instead that violations are independently distributed over
time -- that a violation on one day does not make a violation on the following day more likely;
rejecting this null indicates that violations cluster together in time, which is a more serious
failure than an incorrect overall rate, since it implies the model performs poorly precisely during
extended periods of market stress rather than failing unpredictably. Throughout this thesis, a
$p$-value below the conventional 5\% threshold is treated as evidence against the corresponding null
hypothesis.

Because the volatility target and VaR forecasts in this thesis are constructed over a five-day
horizon while forecasts are produced daily, consecutive VaR windows overlap and share up to four of
their five underlying daily returns. This induces mechanical serial correlation in the violation
series that is unrelated to model quality: a single large market move can register as a violation
across several consecutive, overlapping forecast windows regardless of how well-specified the
underlying volatility model is. The Christoffersen independence test is therefore applied primarily
to a non-overlapping subsample of forecast dates, constructed so that no two consecutive observations
share any underlying daily return; the full-sample result, which reflects this structural overlap
rather than genuine violation clustering, is reported only as a secondary diagnostic.

Expected Shortfall forecasts are subjected to a simple backtest comparing the average realised
shortfall on violation days against the corresponding Expected Shortfall forecast.

The purpose of the backtesting exercise is to determine whether differences in volatility forecasting
accuracy translate into economically relevant differences in market-risk forecasting.

\subsection{Implementation and Reproducibility}
% ============================================================
All models -- econometric, machine learning, and, subsequently, the hybrid specification -- share a
common interface within the walk-forward procedure, so that every model is evaluated through
identical code rather than model-specific logic that could introduce inconsistencies between
families of models. Random Forest and XGBoost are trained with a fixed random seed; an early
diagnostic run also revealed that parallelised training introduced small but non-negligible
floating-point differences across otherwise identical runs, and both models are therefore trained
single-threaded to guarantee exactly reproducible results. Full implementation details are
documented in the accompanying code repository, consistent with keeping the main document readable
independently of the underlying code.

GJR-GARCH outperforms both GARCH(1,1) and EWMA on all three metrics simultaneously, with
improvements ranging from approximately 8--11\% over GARCH(1,1) and 12--22\% over EWMA depending on

allowing it to react more quickly to the volatility increases that typically follow negative
returns -- the well-documented leverage effect in equity index returns -- a pattern that neither
plain GARCH nor EWMA can capture. EWMA, despite having no fitted parameters and no mean-reversion
mechanism, remains within approximately 13\% of GJR-GARCH on RMSE, confirming its role as a
reasonable naive benchmark rather than a negligible one.

These results should be interpreted as a first, partial answer to Research Question 1: among the
econometric models considered, explicitly modelling volatility asymmetry improves out-of-sample
forecasting accuracy. Whether this advantage persists, narrows, or is overturned once the machine
learning models are introduced -- and whether it translates into materially better market-risk
measures -- is assessed in the following subsections.

No single model dominates across all three criteria. XGBoost achieves the lowest MAE of any model
considered, but GJR-GARCH remains the best-performing model under both RMSE and QLIKE. Random

QLIKE penalises most heavily. This pattern -- machine learning improving average-error accuracy
while an asymmetric econometric model retains an advantage under a loss function that specifically
penalises underprediction of risk -- is consistent with Misra et al. (2025), who similarly report

used.

Taken together, these results provide a first, partial answer to Research Question 1: machine
learning does not uniformly outperform the econometric benchmarks, and any apparent improvement is
sensitive to which loss function is used to define ``better'' forecasting performance. This
comparison is revisited following the hybrid model in Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}, and its statistical
significance is formally assessed once all models have been evaluated (Section~\ref{sec:dm}).

\subsection{Market Risk and Backtesting}
% ============================================================
Table~\ref{tab:kupiec_results} reports Kupiec unconditional coverage test results for all five
models at the 95\% and 99\% confidence levels. All models achieve violation rates close to their
nominal levels at 95\%, and all pass the Kupiec test at conventional significance. At 99\%,
XGBoost is the only model that fails: its observed violation rate (1.83\%) is significantly higher
than the nominal 1\% (LR $=4.891$, $p=0.027$), indicating that its VaR forecasts are too narrow at
this stricter confidence level. This is a notable result given XGBoost's performance in
Section~\ref{sec:ml_comparison}: the model that achieves the lowest average forecasting error (MAE)
is also the one whose risk coverage breaks down most clearly at the tail, illustrating that improved
point-forecast accuracy does not automatically translate into improved risk-measure accuracy --
directly relevant to Research Question 1. A plausible explanation is that tree-based models generate
predictions by locally averaging historical outcomes, and therefore struggle to extrapolate beyond
the range of volatility levels observed during training, whereas GARCH-family models extrapolate
parametrically and can, by construction, project conditional variance beyond previously observed
levels. This distinction cannot be confirmed directly from the results reported here, but it is
consistent with the pattern observed in Table~\ref{tab:full_comparison}, where XGBoost's QLIKE --
the metric most sensitive to underprediction of risk -- is also worse than GJR-GARCH's despite
XGBoost's lower MAE.

This thesis set out to evaluate whether machine learning methods improve the out-of-sample
forecasting of financial market volatility relative to traditional econometric models, and whether
any such improvement translates into more accurate market-risk measures.

On forecasting accuracy alone, the results provide a qualified rather than a uniform answer. No
single model dominates across all three loss functions considered. XGBoost achieves the lowest MAE
of any model, indicating that, on an average trading day, it produces the most accurate volatility
forecast. GJR-GARCH, however, remains the best-performing model under both RMSE and QLIKE, and its
advantage over the other models is largest specifically under QLIKE -- the loss function most
sensitive to underprediction of risk. This pattern is consistent with the leverage effect in equity
index returns, which GJR-GARCH's asymmetric specification is designed to capture and which none of
the other models, including the machine learning models, explicitly model.

The market-risk results sharpen this picture rather than simply confirming it. At the 95\% confidence
level, all five models produce well-calibrated VaR forecasts, passing the Kupiec test with violation
rates close to the nominal level. At the stricter 99\% level, however, XGBoost is the only model
that fails: its VaR forecasts are breached significantly more often than the nominal 1\% rate would
imply. This is a substantive finding rather than an incidental one, because XGBoost is simultaneously
the model with the best average forecasting accuracy (MAE) in this thesis. Taken together with its
comparatively weaker QLIKE performance, this suggests that XGBoost's forecasting advantage is
concentrated in ordinary market conditions and does not extend to the extreme tail of the volatility
distribution -- precisely the region that matters most for risk management. This constitutes a
direct, model-specific answer to the second part of Research Question 1: an improvement in
standard point-forecast accuracy does not automatically translate into an improvement in
market-risk measurement, and the two should be evaluated separately rather than assumed to move
together.

Once the mechanical serial correlation induced by the overlapping five-day forecast horizon is
accounted for, none of the five models exhibits genuine clustering of VaR violations: the
Christoffersen independence test, applied to a non-overlapping subsample, is passed comfortably by
every model. Where all five models do share a common weakness is in the magnitude of losses on the
days VaR is actually breached: realised shortfall exceeds forecasted Expected Shortfall for every
model at both confidence levels. Because this pattern is uniform across model families -- econometric
and machine learning alike -- it is best attributed to the shared Gaussian distributional assumption
used to compute VaR and ES throughout this thesis, rather than to a weakness specific to any
individual volatility forecast. This is a known and expected limitation of the modelling choice made
here: financial returns exhibit heavier tails than the Gaussian distribution, and a fat-tailed
alternative (for example, a Student-$t$ distribution) would be expected to narrow, though not
necessarily eliminate, this gap. This is discussed further as a direction for future work below.

Taken together, these results provide a first, partial answer to Research Question 1: machine
learning models can improve average forecasting accuracy relative to econometric benchmarks, but
this improvement is neither uniform across evaluation criteria nor guaranteed to translate into
better-calibrated risk measures. An asymmetric econometric model, GJR-GARCH, remains the strongest
performer under the loss function and confidence level most directly relevant to risk management.
A complete answer to Research Question 1, together with an answer to Research Question 2, requires
the hybrid GARCH--machine learning model and the formal statistical comparison across all six models,
both of which are addressed in the following [sections/subsections to be added].

% -- Limitations paragraph, to be extended once hybrid/DM results are in --
This analysis is subject to several limitations. It is based on a single asset (the S\&P 500) over a
single out-of-sample window (2023--2026), and the extent to which these results generalise to other
assets, markets, or periods is not established here. The market-risk measures rely on a parametric
Gaussian distributional assumption, which Section~\ref{sec:market_risk} shows systematically
understates realised shortfall; a fat-tailed alternative was considered outside the scope of this
thesis but represents a natural extension. Finally, the differences in forecasting performance
reported above have not yet been subjected to a formal test of statistical significance; this is
planned via a Diebold-Mariano test once the hybrid model has been evaluated, so that all model pairs
can be compared in a single, consistent pass.
